\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Transformer families ship as ladders of discrete sizes --- Pythia alone spans
70M to 12B parameters~\citep{biderman2023pythia} --- because different
deployment budgets need different models. Under current practice every rung
is bought separately: each size is a full pretraining run with its own GPU
and energy bill, even though the family members are trained on identical data
with a shared tokenizer and architecture, differing only in width and depth.
The siblings are, by construction, solutions to the same problem at different
capacities; training each from nothing treats them as unrelated. This paper
asks the question the ladder's cost makes practical and its construction
makes tractable: \emph{what actually transfers between two sizes of the same
pretrained model family --- and through what operation can it be carried into
the smaller shape?}

We answer end-to-end on the Pythia suite, where identical data and tokenizer
isolate size as the only variable (\S\ref{sec:setup}), in four controlled
steps. First, we locate the cross-size relation by measuring alignment
separately in representations and in parameters
(\S\ref{sec:representations}): the smaller model's activations are largely a
linear image of the larger model's (held-out ridge $R^2=0.84$), while its
weights are not ($R^2=0.25$--$0.39$ even for embeddings, the cleanest case).
Second, we diagnose why the natural exploit of that alignment --- densely
projecting the large weights into the small shape --- is functionally
destructive (\S\ref{sec:projection}): a bit-exact reconstruction control
rules out assembly, leaving the projection mathematics itself, whose basis
mixing breaks rotary, per-head, GELU, and LayerNorm structure --- blending
destroys the wiring that structured selection preserves. Third, we test
whether what the best-fit linear operator misses is a structured, learnable
correction (\S\ref{sec:residuals}): under shuffle controls it is
statistically indistinguishable from noise, so no zero-shot fix exists at
this granularity. Conversion value must therefore live in
\emph{initialization}, and the fourth step is a matched-budget recovery race
(\S\ref{sec:race}): every candidate init is built without access to the
target's weights and trained under identical data order, schedule, and token
budget. The race decomposes conversion into two independent levers over
structure-respecting selection --- closed-form least-squares
\emph{compensation}, which repairs what the initial network computes, and
variance-preserving \emph{rescale}, which repairs the scale the optimizer
sees --- and shows that, because they act on disjoint weight paths, they
stack.

Our contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{A controlled characterization of what transfers across sizes.}
  Within a family trained on identical data, representations align strongly
  under a linear map (held-out ridge $R^2=0.84$) while parameters align
  weakly ($R^2=0.25$--$0.39$ in the cleanest, embedding case) --- the gap the
  rest of the paper pivots on (\S\ref{sec:representations}).
\item \textbf{A mechanically verified diagnosis of why dense projection
  fails.} Rebuilding the donor from its own extracted weights reproduces its
  logits bit-exactly, so the projected model's collapse is attributable to
  basis mixing alone: dense maps break rotary pairing, per-head attention,
  elementwise GELU, and per-feature LayerNorm --- with the LayerNorm mismatch
  isolated as the largest single factor (\S\ref{sec:projection}).
\item \textbf{A null result under controls.} After the best-fit linear
  operator, weight residuals carry no cross-layer direction, no
  patch-learnable signal, and no cross-pair transfer (spectral, consistency,
  learnability, and transfer tests against shuffle controls with bootstrap
  confidence intervals, $p\ge0.99$): a learned zero-shot correction is not
  available at this granularity (\S\ref{sec:residuals}).
\item \textbf{A two-lever decomposition of conversion, and a method that
  stacks them.} At matched budget, least-squares compensation is the
  \emph{function} lever (best zero-shot) and variance-preserving rescale is
  the \emph{training-dynamics} lever (best endpoints); their combination ---
  Compensated Selection --- dominates the strongest variant of weight
  subcloning~\citep{samragh2023weight} on both axes and in every seed
  ($84.0\pm1.8$ vs.\ $89.7\pm3.7$ final perplexity, 3/3 paired wins;
  zero-shot $18.5$k vs.\ $61.9$k) and finishes 18$\times$ better than from
  scratch at 30M tokens; the ordering holds out-of-domain and at $2\times$
  the training context, and the margin over from scratch
  ($\sim$13$\times$) generalizes with three seeds to a held-out
  depth-dominated pair (\S\ref{sec:race}).
\end{itemize}
