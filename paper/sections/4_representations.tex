\section{Representations align, parameters do not}
\label{sec:representations}

A size conversion presupposes that the two family members are related; the
question is \emph{where} the relation lives. On the primary width-only pair
(1.4B$\,\to\,$410M, same depth and head count; \S\ref{sec:setup}) we measure
the relation in two places --- the representations both models compute, and the
parameters they store --- and find it in only one of them.

\paragraph{Layer correspondence saturates under CKA.}
Linear CKA between all $24\times24$ layer pairs, computed on the similarity
subset (all token positions; \S\ref{sec:setup}), gives a diagonal mean of
$0.883$, yet only $12\%$ of layers place their single best match exactly on the
diagonal. The reason is visible in Figure~\ref{fig:align} (left): the heatmap is
one large saturated block --- layers 0--2 distinct, the middle band (layers
$\sim$4--22) mutually $\approx1.0$, layer 23 distinct. Linear CKA therefore
confirms only \emph{coarse} early/mid/late correspondence and saturates in the
middle band (a known limitation of the metric), so it is not a sharp per-layer
discriminator. This motivates a directional, quantitative map: how much of the
small model's representation is a linear image of the large model's?

\paragraph{Activations are largely a linear image across widths.}
Per layer, we fit a large$\,\to\,$small map on pooled activations (8k train /
2k held-out) and score held-out $R^2$ under two operators of increasing
freedom: \emph{Procrustes} (a rotation plus one global scale) and \emph{ridge}
(a full linear map $2048\!\to\!1024$). Ridge reaches a mean held-out
$R^2=0.844$ (Table~\ref{tab:align}): the 410M pooled representation is largely a
linear projection of the 1.4B's, capturing $84\%$ of held-out variance.
Procrustes already reaches $0.717$, so most of the relationship is a change of
basis; the $\sim$0.13 gap is genuine non-orthogonal reshaping. Both maps are
discriminative per layer and dip together at layers 3--5 ---
the same layers CKA flagged as distinct (Figure~\ref{fig:align}, right).

\paragraph{Raw parameters do not.}
The token embeddings are the one weight matrix where the two sizes share an axis
(the vocabulary) and differ only in width, so a single map applies; we split
rows (tokens) $80/20$. Even in this cleanest case a linear map explains only
$0.25$--$0.39$ of weight variance (Table~\ref{tab:align}): ridge $R^2=0.248$ on
the input embedding and $0.386$ on the output embedding, against $0.844$ for
activations.

\paragraph{The representation--parameter gap.}
Two independently trained members of a family arrive at closely related
\emph{representations} while their \emph{parameters} are only loosely a linear
map of one another. This gap is the pivot of the paper. If representations align
but weights do not, then projecting the large model's weights into the small
model's shape (\S\ref{sec:projection}) must leave a substantial residual, and
whether that residual is structured and learnable or merely noise
(\S\ref{sec:residuals}) decides where conversion value can come from.
